# Build log (one line per session: the date, what shipped, what I learned)

- 2026-10-22: Audited the paper and the authors' code. The over-provisioning metric divides by about 1e-10, and spike precision rewards forecasts that lag. Wrote the review. Learned: Reading the evaluation code revealed that extreme over-provisioning values, near-zero usage, and permissive spike matching could distort the paper's reported performance. The tables showed the results, but not whether the metrics measured what they claimed to measure. In the next paper, I would check the evaluation code first, especially metric definitions, edge cases, and baseline scoring.
- 2026-10-22 (later): Streamed the 29 GB Alibaba container trace into 5-minute bins (5,946 traces after filtering), rebuilt the preprocessing and trained the authors' model on three targets. Reproduced the headline result (spike F1 0.42 → 0.65 on their own evaluator) and the metric artefact. A plain classifier read at an upper quantile matches the proposed method's recall with about 30% less over-provisioning, but loses under a 15-minute actuation delay. The train.py from this run was changed later, so it isn't in the history. Learned: The ranking changed with the delay, showing that no single score captures a method's performance under every operating condition. A method that performs best without delay may not be best in a realistic delayed-response setting. Methods should therefore be compared across relevant operating conditions and practical costs, not just one headline metric.
- 2026-10-22 to 23: Cut training memory from 3.9 GB to 2.5 GB, then trained a 25-minute look-ahead target without the hold, three seeds per target. The look-ahead alone beats the paper's target at every matched over-provisioning level (+8 points of spike coverage and 19% less throttling at slack 0.6), so holding the allocation after a spike costs more than it gives. Learned: When an idea combines multiple changes, test each change independently through controlled ablation experiments. Otherwise, it is impossible to tell which component helps, which hurts, and which makes no difference. Separating the look-ahead from the holding period revealed that the look-ahead helped while the holding period reduced performance.
- 2026-10-23: Added a 30-minute look-ahead target to test whether matching the look-ahead to the delay restores coverage at 30 minutes; training started. Set up this repo: portable runners, a setup step that pins the authors' code, and these process files. Learned: I would initialize Git before writing any experimental code, commit a working baseline, and commit each meaningful change separately. I would also preserve configurations, random seeds, dependencies, and experiment outputs. This would make the results reproducible and eliminate the need to reconstruct earlier versions from file timestamps.
- 2026-10-23 (later): The 30-minute look-ahead brings spike coverage back at a 30-minute delay: 0.74 against 0.59 for the 25-minute target at slack 0.8, in all three seeds, with no measurable cost at shorter delays. Wrote the architecture diagrams, seven ADRs, the README and the project story, and gave every document a plainer-wording pass. Learned: Testing the prediction showed that a plausible trade-off is still only a hypothesis until experiments support it. The longer look-ahead substantially improved coverage at a 30-minute delay without a measurable penalty at shorter delays across three seeds. This also showed that the paper's recall metric could rank methods differently from their actual performance under delayed response, reinforcing the need to evaluate methods under the conditions they are designed to handle.
